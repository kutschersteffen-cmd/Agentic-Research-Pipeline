# ASSAY

*A visual philosophy*

---

Assay begins with a refusal. It refuses the assertion, the gesture made without
evidence, the surface that asks to be believed rather than examined. In its place it
offers the patient apparatus of testing: the sample divided, the fraction weighed, the
residue named. This is an aesthetic of things that have been *put through something* — a
worldview in which beauty is not applied to a surface but survives a process. Nothing in
an Assay composition may simply appear. Every mark must have been earned, and the work
must carry, in its bones, the evidence of the hours that produced it. The hand behind it
is a hand at the absolute top of its field, and the viewer must feel that before they
understand anything else.

**Space and form.** The governing structure is the plate — a bounded field of
observation, generously margined, surrounded by quiet. Within it, form arrives as
accumulation rather than statement: hundreds or thousands of small, identical marks laid
down in strict ranks, each one nearly nothing, together becoming a weather. The eye first
reads the field as texture, then as population, then — on the third or fourth pass —
begins to find the minority within it: the few marks that differ, that were selected,
that failed. This is the central pleasure of the movement. Meaning is not delivered; it
is *discovered*, and only by the viewer willing to stay. Such fields cannot be
approximated or faked. They must be built mark by meticulous mark, and their rightness
depends on a positional discipline maintained across every single unit.

**Color and material.** The palette is narrow to the point of austerity — a ground the
color of aged paper or unbleached linen, an ink that is nearly but never quite black, and
at most two reagents: one cool and deep, one oxidised and warm. These two are the only
colors permitted to *mean* anything, and they are spent with extreme parsimony, appearing
on perhaps one mark in ten, one mark in a hundred. Color in Assay is not decoration and
not mood; it is a result — the stain that appears when a thing has reacted. Because it is
rationed so severely, a single warm mark in a field of grey ink carries the weight that a
lesser work would need a whole panel to achieve. Every hue must be calibrated with
painstaking care; an approximate color destroys the entire proposition.

**Scale and rhythm.** Assay works at two scales and deliberately omits the middle. There
is the microscopic — hairline rules, tick marks, numerals set small enough to require
leaning in, registration marks and plate numbers in the margins — and there is the
monumental: one line of text, perhaps four words, set impossibly fine and impossibly
large, occupying its own register of silence. Nothing occupies the comfortable middle
ground, because the middle is where design goes to be merely competent. The rhythm
between these two scales is the music of the form: dense, dense, dense, then a long
exhalation. Achieving this without the composition tearing in half is the labor of the
movement, and it is considerable.

**Composition and balance.** The underlying grid is orthogonal, strict, and largely
invisible — the discipline of a scientific plate rather than the play of a poster. Into
it, a single curve or diagonal may be admitted: one gesture of motion across the static
field, drawn as a hairline, carrying a band of uncertainty around it like a breath. This
lone non-orthogonal element must be earned; it is the only place where the work permits
itself to suggest that something is *changing*. Balance comes from the tension between
the immovable ranks and this one traveling line — between the census and the trajectory.
Margins are wide and inviolable. Nothing crowds an edge. Nothing overlaps. The restraint
must read as mastery, never as emptiness.

**Visual hierarchy and text.** Language enters Assay as apparatus, not as explanation.
Small mono numerals label axes, number plates, count fractions, cite a date — the
vocabulary of someone recording rather than persuading. These are whisper-quiet and
carefully positioned, and the viewer may ignore them entirely without losing the work.
Above them, one phrase is permitted. It is short, declarative, and set as the single
typographic event of the composition — the conclusion the whole apparatus was built to
support. There are never paragraphs. There is never a caption that explains the image;
the image is not illustrating an argument, it *is* the argument. The final work should
look as though it was labored over for weeks by someone who knew precisely what they were
withholding — every alignment refined, every interval tested, every mark placed with
master-level deliberation, and then the entire thing quietly stripped back until only
what was necessary remained.
